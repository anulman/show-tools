# Character dance

Make a dance that suits the character. Establish the motion with a few coherent drawings and a held-pose animatic before adding in-betweens or expanding the cast.

## Ground the character and scope

Inspect the approved art and the current design file. Identify the character's proportions, palette, props, attitude, view, and active fidelity. Read any supplied recording with the Scrawl skill before making changes.

Describe the intended dance in one sentence. For a quiet default loop, consider a small knee dip, hip shift, foot tap, or head bop. Feet may move. Judge plausible weight and contact rather than imposing a foot lock. Preserve stronger movement when the user requests it.

Keep joint articulation distinct from whole-body effects. Scaling or lifting an unchanged sprite can make a useful jump or squash effect, but it does not create a bent knee, weight shift, or dance gesture. Draw or rig the body changes the requested motion needs.

Choose contacts and joints from the character's geometry. A slug or snail can keep its supporting belly strip planted while the upper body ripples and the eye stalks nod. Keep its rigid shell coherent. Do not force humanoid knee motion onto a creature without knees. Preserve the approved silhouette, face and characteristic prop scale during a repaint; crisp new pixels do not excuse changing a tuba into a narrow horn or losing a defining shell.

Use Figma as the primary authoring and review workspace unless the user chooses another destination. Load the current Figma use and motion skills before calling those tools, plus the component guidance when creating pose components. Inspect actual IDs and conventions. Local PNGs are preparation inputs; MP4s are secondary review exports. Do not replace the Figma deliverable with local PDFs. If the chosen workspace is unavailable, continue useful preparation and report the connection gap. Do not claim delivery or silently create a different workspace.

For a new motion direction, start with one character. Expand only within the user's requested scope. A request to write this skill does not authorize generating the rest of the cast.

## Add a stronger dance variant

When the user requests more vigorous moves, retain the approved quiet default and create a separately named variant. Describe the additional action before changing amplitude. A deeper knee opening, hip counter-shift, baton cut, wider wing pulse or heel-toe rock can express effort. Faster playback or a larger global bob does not create a new move.

Increase one or two readable actions while preserving the character's physical temperament and stable identity pieces. Name the support transfers and free limbs. Review the assembled joints at the larger extremes; exact rigid-piece reuse alone cannot prevent a shoulder or leg root from separating. Extend proximal geometry behind moving pieces as needed.

Use additional drawings only when the stronger gesture needs them. Keep poses and phase offsets in beats, independent of movie frame rate. The new variant requires its own drawing hashes, both visual verdicts and an actual authoring-workspace export review. A failed energetic variant must not replace or inherit approval from the quiet default.

## Illustrate special-move animatics

For a written special-move concept, illustrate its defining action before adding stage effects. Use a neutral entry, anticipation, readable accent, recovery and neutral exit. A quiet dance with a new name or a particle effect does not establish a tail sweep, conducting stop or support transfer. Preserve native character scale when enlarging the canvas for prop arcs; record the source offset, pivot and movement footprint.

Keep one-shot action duration separate from the review cycle. Explicit beat keys can hold a finished action in neutral before the next repetition. Verify those nonuniform holds against the authored keys and actual decoded drawings, rather than assuming four equal dance phases. Keep character and optional effect layers separate so a floating accent cannot excuse a broken limb. Update the concept's existing review card with its actual animated source and retain linked pose components for programmatic use.

## Explore in parallel

Use the user's requested number of explorations. Three contrasting, bounded candidates are a useful default for an open dance brief. When delegation is available, give each subagent its own output directory and a complete shared brief. One agent owns Figma writes.

Share identity references, fidelity, camera and scale constraints, motion intent, current corrections, and deliverables. Give each candidate a different gesture or rhythm focus. Avoid generating three intensity levels of the same idea when the user wants broader exploration.

Start with roughly four key drawings when that can express the gesture. The number is a starting budget, not a requirement for every character. Ask for successive moments in one loop, not unrelated stance concepts. Use the imagegen skill for generated raster drawings. Existing editable rigs may be animated directly.

Each candidate returns the drawings, a contact sheet, a playable held-pose animatic, pose meanings, timing in beats, exact generation inputs, preparation settings, and known flaws. Propagate user steering to every active worker. For an approved motion process applied to more characters, reuse the validated approach rather than automatically repeating an expensive three-way search for each one.

## Prepare without erasing motion

Use a common camera and scale across drawings. Preserve the actual lowering pelvis, bending legs, foot movement, and head follow-through. Independently fitting each pose to the same height can erase the dance or introduce breathing scale.

Register stable screen space and plausible ground contact. Use the project's canvas, palette, alpha, pixel grid, and source anchors. Do not hardcode Gator's dimensions for another project or character. Inspect each drawing for clipping, identity drift, changed face direction, props, and proportion changes. Pixel hashes prove distinct files, not distinct or correct poses.

## Review and synthesize

Watch every candidate loop and inspect its drawings. Compare character appropriateness, requested restraint, articulation, identity consistency, registration/contact, rhythm, and loop closure. A static contact sheet cannot establish timing.

Decompose promising candidates into ideas: gesture, weight shift, foot action, head or prop follow-through, amplitude, cadence, holds, and staging. Consider combinations deliberately. Do not treat this as a winner-only contest.

Build a small hybrid animatic when a combination could improve the result. For example, try one candidate's knee dip with another's foot tap and a third's head timing. Borrowing timing requires retiming the drawings; borrowing an anatomical gesture may require redrawing the chosen character family or editing its rig. Combine compatible drawings directly when they already share view, scale, and identity. Reconcile incompatible drawings into a coherent final rather than splicing visible style changes.

Compare the hybrid against the strongest original. Keep the combination when it improves the requested dance. A single candidate can still be the best result, but explain which combinations were considered or tried and why they did not help. Cross-judging is useful for a substantial exploration; the main agent still inspects the work and owns the decision.

Do not equate a slower playback rate with a smaller gesture. Reduce an oversized gesture in the drawings or rig when necessary. Use holds and beat placement to shape rhythm. Add tween drawings only where the animatic reveals a needed transition. Continuous smoothing is not the default for a choppy cartoon or pixel-art brief.

Distinguish articulation from redraw noise. Name the moving joints, rigid pieces, support contacts and intentional highlights before polishing. Reuse the same face, hair, shell or prop pixels when that part only translates; do not regenerate or shade it differently in every pose. Use integer pixel edits or native vector pieces for small articulations. A silhouette change should explain a joint or perspective change. Detail flicker that merely suggests turning reads as a rendering defect; use a coherent stable piece or draw a clearly readable turn.

Review local details over time, not just the overall silhouette or a contact sheet. Align a rigid part across poses and compare its pixel clusters. Check that the supporting foot remains in world space when the dance calls for a planted stance; other feet may tap or step. Reserve shimmer for deliberate, bounded accents such as one disco-ball glint in one held pose, rather than changing skin or material shading throughout the loop.

Keep connected joints continuous across every part offset. Extend leg roots or other proximal segments behind a moving body with sufficient overlap, then inspect the attachment at the motion extremes. Check the assembled silhouette for transparent cuts as well as checking each piece's texture stability; a horizontal cut mask needs an overlapping join when its pieces move independently.

Trace each moving limb through its named joints and terminal part in every pose and across the drawing changes. A sprite can remain one connected silhouette while a cut mask leaves an extra shoe, a dangling toe stub or a knee that leads into the wrong ankle. Do not split crossed or bent limbs with an arbitrary vertical or horizontal crop. Draw or isolate the complete anatomical chain, then check the intended number of appendages, ankle-to-shoe attachment, foot orientation and support transfer at native size and in a zoomed loop. A character-specific material or endpoint check can catch this failure when whole-silhouette connectivity cannot; prove it rejects the actual bad keyframe and retain the visual anatomy review. Compose specialized limb checks with the existing silhouette checks rather than replacing them. Explicitly account for intentional floating accents before allowing extra components.

## Build and verify the final

### Repaint before production use

Concept art may use painting, diffusion, or style transfer. Every selected drawing entering a finished sprite-sheet or video pipeline requires a deliberate repaint and a fresh style review. An animatic that establishes the dance is still a concept until this step passes.

Repaint the approved motion in the project's intended pixel-art style. Preserve the pose, camera, character identity, common scale, registration and timing. Use deliberate pixel clusters, clear silhouettes, consistent outlines and stepped colour ramps. Keep faces, costume marks and props consistent across drawings. Remove painted blur, accidental anti-aliasing, noisy near-colours, smeared details and redraw shimmer. Use the approved fidelity and palette intent; do not impose a universal palette cap or lower the resolution to hide defects.

Ask exactly: **"Is this the work of a mid-res 8-bit pixel artist?"** Inspect every selected pose at native logical resolution and an integer nearest-neighbour enlargement, then inspect the loop. Prefer a fresh reviewer for a finished family. The answer must be **pass** or **needs repaint**, with visible evidence. A pixel grid, binary alpha, image-generation prompt, palette count, or timing check cannot answer that question. Do not relabel a resized or quantized concept as a repaint. Repair and re-review failures before promoting the family.

Record the approved drawing hashes, logical grid, palette treatment, reviewer verdict and evidence in the project. Invalidate the verdict when artwork changes. Keep concept alternatives clearly labelled and outside finished exports; they do not need production repaint unless selected.

Also ask: **"Does this look like intentional movement of the same pixel model, rather than texture crawl or distortion?"** Record a separate temporal verdict tied to the drawing hashes. A still-art pass cannot establish this. Use stable-part pixel comparisons or support-contact checks where the authored movement makes those exact invariants meaningful; verify them against an actual rejected loop before relying on them.

For a rigid-part or contact crop, verify that the selected region contains the intended visible pixels and record its occupied coverage. Matching hashes of transparent background do not prove stability. Name the actual part and support for each creature rather than inheriting another character's crop or anatomy labels.

Verify the rendered result too. Use integer pixel scales and aligned anchors, and prevent interpolation from smearing clean source pixels. When the authoring tool cannot preserve raster sampling, a lossless conversion of an already approved pixel master into native pixel geometry is an option. That conversion does not replace repainting. Decode the actual video and inspect close-ups as well as the scene. A source-art pass does not excuse blurred delivery.

Promote a family to finished only when both its repaint/style review and its export review pass. Record this status with the exact artwork version so scripts can refuse unreviewed assets. Report an unfinished family as a concept instead of calling it a finished sprite sheet or video.

Put the candidates and synthesized final in the chosen review workspace. In Figma, use the current review area and give the final concrete pose components or an editable rig. Record an explicit sequence, beat phases, cycle length, and the intended starting tempo. Keep the cast-specific dance separate from the underlying artwork and musical clock.

For a drawn-pose animatic, held opacity swaps are a simple implementation. Verify exactly one drawing is visible at every change and at closure. For a rig, inspect the articulated motion rather than substituting an unrelated global bounce. Keep beat phase separate from rendered frame count so tempo and offbeat offsets can be changed without rewriting the art.

Export and decode the timeline from the actual authoring workspace. For Figma, verify its exported timeline rather than only a separately assembled local movie. Inspect every drawing change, repeated cycles, and closure. Static screenshots show layout, not animation. Check default character-sheet usage against the chosen final. Update references and retire exact superseded study IDs within the authorized cleanup scope. Retain current comparison alternatives when they help the user's review.

Keep diagnostics out of performance exports. Timing dots, phase tickers, comparison labels and mock meters belong in an explicitly requested diagnostic view. Leave configurable HUD and text slots available, but default unused slots to hidden or empty. Do not invent scene slogans, lyric text or gameplay semantics to fill them. Inspect the clean rendered scene as its own deliverable.

On explicit promotion, Figma is canonical for artwork and animation. Verify that its pose geometry, required named body/effect layers and timeline can supply the required exports. Verify every pixel in enlarged native cells and transparent registration margins; centre-only downsampling can hide stray geometry or blur. Check transparency and native registration: a canvas-background preview is not a transparent sprite export. Retain stable semantic identities, Figma file/node references, native dimensions, offsets/pivots, beat keys, exact runtime export hashes and compact style/temporal/anatomy verdicts bound to those pixels. A later re-export must match that certificate or return to review. Runtime packages must work offline without raw review reports.

Keep candidates, contact sheets, recordings and detailed process evidence temporary. At milestone handoff or housekeeping, extract useful lessons into existing docs, parameters or meaningful tests; retain only minimal regression fixtures a maintained check needs. Inactive process evidence may expire after verified canonical handoff and dependency migration. Intentionally abandoned prototypes may expire after their lessons are recorded. Protect active work, awaiting-promotion inputs, shared reports still needed by other families, code and required runtime exports. Do not impose a permanent provenance archive or a blanket purge. A compact promotion receipt supersedes raw approval reports only after the consuming validator has migrated to it.

Keep exploratory animation distinct from pixel-clean production art and live engine integration. Report the Figma final, secondary playable export, synthesis choices and material limitations.

Read [the Gator case](references/gator-case.md) when a worked example would help with motion restraint, timing, or interpreting earlier feedback. Treat its numbers and outcome as evidence from one character, not universal defaults.
